The modern psychedelic research era is usually dated to a single 2006 paper. Two decades on, it is no longer a small, embattled specialism: it has FDA breakthrough therapy designations, dedicated university research centres funded by tens of millions of philanthropic dollars, a candidate account of its own neural mechanism, and randomised trial data showing large, sustained effects across depression, cancer-related distress, and PTSD. It has also produced a regulatory rejection despite positive Phase 3 data, an aggressive patent dispute over who gets to own a centuries-old fungus's therapeutic use, and a growing, unresolved argument about whether the field's own history, covered in a companion piece on this site, is repeating itself. This piece covers what the current trial evidence shows, the mechanism now under active investigation, and the commercial, regulatory, and ethical questions that evidence has not settled.

What the Trials Actually Show

Roland Griffiths and colleagues' 2006 paper, "Psilocybin can occasion mystical-type experiences having substantial and sustained personal meaning and spiritual significance", is widely credited with reopening the field to serious institutional attention. The study gave thirty psychiatrically healthy volunteers a high dose of psilocybin under supportive, carefully controlled conditions and found that most rated the experience among the most personally and spiritually significant of their lives, with positive changes in mood and outlook still present at follow-up. It was not, on its own, a treatment trial, but it demonstrated that a classic psychedelic could be administered to volunteers under modern research governance without the loss of methodological control that had dogged parts of the 1960s literature, and it gave funders and regulators a template to build on.

The treatment trials that followed have tested that template against real clinical populations. Two independent randomised, double-blind trials published together in 2016, led respectively by Griffiths' group at Johns Hopkins and Stephen Ross' group at NYU, gave psilocybin to patients with life-threatening cancer diagnoses and clinically significant depression or anxiety, comparing a high dose against a low, placebo-like dose in a crossover design. Both trials found large, rapid reductions in depression and anxiety after a single high-dose session, with roughly sixty to eighty per cent of participants classified as clinical responders at six-month follow-up, an effect size well beyond what conventional antidepressants typically achieve in comparable populations. Separate pilot work has extended the approach to smoking cessation and to other substance-use indications with promising, though smaller-scale, results. On the MDMA side, two Phase 3 trials of MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD, run by MAPS' public-benefit drug-development arm, reported positive results on the trials' primary outcome measures, clearing the bar Phase 3 trials are designed to test. The FDA nonetheless declined to approve the resulting New Drug Application in August 2024, citing concerns about the trials' design, including the practical impossibility of blinding participants to whether they had received a drug with such unmistakable subjective effects, and about safety monitoring and reported boundary violations in some trial-adjacent therapy settings. Positive headline trial results and regulatory approval remain two different hurdles, and the MDMA decision is the clearest recent evidence that the second one has not become a formality.

A Candidate Mechanism: The Entropic Brain

Alongside the clinical trials, neuroimaging work led by Robin Carhart-Harris and collaborators has built a candidate account of why a single psychedelic session might produce effects that persist for months. Functional imaging studies of LSD and psilocybin consistently show reduced activity and connectivity within the default mode network, a set of interconnected brain regions active during self-referential thought, rumination, and mind-wandering, alongside altered activity in subcortical hubs such as the thalamus. Carhart-Harris' entropic brain hypothesis frames this as an increase in the brain's overall neural entropy, a shift toward a less constrained, more flexible pattern of activity, of the kind also seen, in different form, during REM sleep and early psychosis. On this account, conditions such as depression are associated with an overly rigid, self-focused pattern of brain activity, and a psychedelic-induced disruption of that pattern, particularly when it produces a subjectively significant, ego-dissolving mystical-type experience, can act as a kind of reset that outlasts the drug's own pharmacological half-life by months.

That account connects directly to the receptor pharmacology discussed in a companion piece on this site: 5-HT2A agonism is the initiating molecular event, but the mystical-type experience and default mode network disruption are downstream consequences that current trial data suggest may be doing the actual therapeutic work. Across several of the trials above, the subjective intensity of the mystical-type experience during the session correlates with the size of the later clinical improvement, which is either a genuine mechanistic clue or a significant complication, depending on how it is interpreted. If a sufficiently intense subjective experience turns out to be mechanistically necessary, then the biased, non-hallucinogenic 5-HT2A agonists discussed in that companion piece, designed specifically to avoid producing such an experience, may end up unable to reproduce the same clinical effect even if they activate the same receptor. That question is unresolved, and it is one of the more consequential open problems in the field.

An Institutional Field, Not a Fringe One

The clearest sign that psychedelic research has moved from the margins into the scientific establishment is where the money and the buildings now are. Imperial College London opened what is generally regarded as the world's first dedicated psychedelic research centre in April 2019, backed by roughly $3.5 million in private funding. Johns Hopkins followed five months later, launching its Center for Psychedelic and Consciousness Research in September 2019 on the strength of $17 million in philanthropic gifts, with Griffiths as founding director, the first centre of its kind in the United States. Other institutions, including NYU and UCLA, built out parallel research programmes over the same period. What is notable about all of this is the funding source: this entire institutional rebuild ran on private philanthropy rather than government science budgets, a direct legacy of Schedule I status making federal grant funding for human hallucinogen trials difficult to obtain even as the clinical evidence accumulated. A field can acquire university buildings, endowed chairs, and a respected publication record while still sitting outside the normal funding infrastructure that most comparably promising areas of medicine take for granted.

Commercialisation, Patents, and Who Benefits

Institutional respectability has attracted commercial capital, and commercial capital has produced its own controversy. Compass Pathways, initially established as a charitable venture, converted to a for-profit structure and listed publicly in 2020, and has since pursued an aggressive patent strategy around a proprietary synthetic psilocybin formulation and its methods of clinical use. A coalition operating as Freedom to Operate has formally challenged several of those patents, arguing that the underlying chemistry and therapeutic approach are not novel inventions but adaptations of practices already documented in Indigenous and underground therapeutic use for decades. The Mazatec communities of Oaxaca, whose ceremonial use of Psilocybe mushrooms brought the compound to Western scientific attention in the first place, have received no share of the resulting commercial value, a pattern critics describe as biopiracy: the commercial capture of Indigenous knowledge without acknowledgement or compensation. Not every organisation in the field has taken the same route; the nonprofit Usona Institute has developed its own synthetic psilocybin manufacturing process and published it openly rather than patenting it, on the explicit reasoning that broad, unencumbered access serves the field's therapeutic goals better than proprietary control does. The tension between the two models, that expensive Phase 3 trial programmes plausibly need some prospect of commercial return to attract capital at all, against the discomfort of a small number of companies controlling access to a therapy built on centuries of uncompensated Indigenous practice, remains entirely unresolved.

Regulatory Tension and the Limits of Enthusiasm

Federal and state law currently point in opposite directions. LSD and psilocybin remain Schedule I substances under US federal law, the same category assigned in 1970, even as Oregon's licensed psilocybin therapy framework and Colorado's broader Natural Medicine Health Act create state-level legal access to substances federal law still classifies as having no accepted medical use. The FDA's 2024 rejection of MDMA-assisted therapy, despite two positive Phase 3 trials, is a reminder that the regulatory apparatus sitting above both state initiatives and the clinical trial pipeline has not been carried along by enthusiasm for the field's promise. Some researchers and commentators have begun describing an opposite risk explicitly, sometimes under the label psychedelic exceptionalism: the tendency, visible in some clinical, media, and investment coverage of this field, to treat encouraging early results as settled proof and to wave away exactly the kind of design limitations, small sample sizes, functional unblinding, and short follow-up windows, that would receive routine scrutiny in any other area of drug development. Given the history covered in the companion piece on this site, in which advocacy outrunning evidence contributed materially to the field's collapse the first time, that caution is not an incidental footnote to the modern research programme. It is close to the central lesson the field drew from its own history.

What Remains Unresolved

Several open questions sit underneath the field's positive headline results. Most published trials follow participants for weeks or months, not years, so how long a single or a few sessions' benefit lasts beyond the published follow-up windows is not yet established. Whether the mystical-type subjective experience is mechanistically required for the therapeutic effect, discussed above, bears directly on whether non-hallucinogenic alternatives can ever substitute for it. The current therapy model is intensive: extended preparatory and integration sessions either side of a multi-hour, one-to-one supervised dosing session, a structure that does not scale easily against the number of patients who might benefit and the limited pool of appropriately trained therapists. Cost and access questions follow directly from that scaling problem, and the commercial and Indigenous-rights tensions described above are not incidental to it. Finally, the same trial evidence that supports supervised clinical use says little about the safety of the much larger population using these compounds recreationally or in unsupervised underground therapeutic settings, where none of the screening, monitoring, or integration support shown to matter in the trials is reliably present.

A Field Being Careful on Purpose

Two decades after Griffiths' 2006 study, psychedelic research has clearly moved from curiosity to a credible, institutionally backed area of clinical medicine, with trial results in several serious indications that most areas of psychiatry would be glad to have. It has done so, visibly, more slowly and more cautiously than the pace of the underlying results alone might justify, a direct and reasonable response to a history in which the same substances were oversold once already. The open questions that remain, mechanism, durability, access, and the proper relationship between rigorous evidence and public enthusiasm, are not signs the field is failing. They are closer to evidence that this time, unlike the first, the caution is being applied before the collapse rather than after it.
