Psilocybin mushrooms, ergot fungus, and the peyote and San Pedro cacti share no recent common ancestor, no habitat, and no plausible reason to have evolved with a primate audience in mind. Each independently manufactures a molecule that binds, with striking precision, to a single human neural receptor: the serotonin 2A receptor, 5-HT2A. That precision looks designed. It is not. The receptor these compounds activate is older than the vertebrate lineage, conserved across an evolutionary distance that predates the split between animals with backbones and animals without one. What looks like a lock built for a particular key is closer to a lock that has sat in the door for six hundred million years, waiting for whatever happened to fit it. This piece follows that coincidence from its molecular mechanics through to the societies it has since reshaped: why the organisms that make these compounds were never under any selective pressure to affect a human mind, the specific evolutionary and historical footprint left by ergot alkaloids, what recent cryo-electron microscopy has revealed about how these molecules engage the receptor, and how an accident of deep homology went on to steer Mesoamerican ritual practice, a 1960s countercultural movement, decades of prohibition, and a live clinical research programme now working through Phase 2 and 3 trials.

An Ancient Lock, Not a Bespoke One

The obvious question raised by any of the classic psychedelics, psilocybin from Psilocybe mushrooms, DMT from a wide scatter of unrelated plant genera, mescaline from peyote and San Pedro, and LSD's own ergoline scaffold from a grass pathogen, is why a molecule built by a fungus or a cactus should have any coherent effect on a human mind at all. The answer starts with the receptor rather than the molecule. 5-HT2A belongs to the serotonin receptor superfamily, a set of G-protein-coupled receptor lineages that had already diverged from one another before the ancestors of vertebrates and the ancestors of invertebrates split, a divergence dated to roughly 600 million years ago. Recognisable homologues of the mammalian 5-HT1, 5-HT2, 5-HT4, 5-HT6, and 5-HT7 receptor families turn up across an enormous span of animal life: in planarians, in molluscs, in decapod crustaceans, in insects. The receptor family is not a human invention, or even a vertebrate one. It is closer to a piece of shared animal infrastructure that happened to be sitting in place for hundreds of millions of years before the plants and fungi discussed here ever existed.

That antiquity matters for the coincidence framing this piece is built around. A defensive chemical that happens to target something as fundamental and conserved as a 600-million-year-old signalling receptor is, in a real sense, aiming at the broadest possible surface: almost any animal nervous system will have some version of the lock that chemical fits, whether the organism doing the biting or grazing is an insect, a gastropod, a bird, or eventually a primate. There was no co-evolution between Claviceps purpurea and Homo sapiens, nor between Psilocybe and Homo sapiens; the two lineages of organisms never shared a niche in any sense that would let natural selection tune one to the other. What they shared, at a much deeper level, was a target already built into the architecture of animal nervous systems long before either lineage arose. Humans are not the reason these molecules work. Humans are another instance of the old, general kind of nervous system these molecules were, in an evolutionary sense, already equipped to disrupt.

Chemical Warfare, Not Charity

None of this is charity on the part of the fungus. The leading hypotheses for why Psilocybe mushrooms make psilocybin at all are defensive. The oldest version of the argument holds that psilocybin deters insect grazing: fungal tissue is a nutritionally valuable target for a wide range of invertebrate predators, and a compound that measurably impairs an insect's feeding behaviour, survival, or development is exactly the kind of trait natural selection would favour in a stationary, undefended fruiting body. Early scepticism about this account noted that psilocybin-containing mushrooms are regularly found hosting apparently healthy insect larvae, which looked like a problem for a straightforward deterrence story. More recent experimental work, extracting psilocybin and testing its effect on insect survival, development, and behaviour directly, has found effects consistent with a genuine defensive role, even if larvae of some tolerant species can still exploit the tissue.

A newer and more specific hypothesis targets gastropods rather than insects. Molecular dating places the divergence of the Psilocybe lineage at roughly 65 million years ago, close to the Cretaceous-Palaeogene extinction event, and terrestrial gastropods, slugs and snails, underwent a substantial diversification in the aftermath of that extinction, becoming significant fungal predators. On this account psilocybin's origin tracks the rise of a specific predator guild rather than insects generally. A further, non-competing possibility is that psilocybin's psychoactive effects alter the behaviour of the animals that do eat the mushroom in ways that favour spore dispersal, turning an initially defensive compound into an incidental aid to reproduction once an animal is affected rather than deterred outright. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the genus's evolutionary history plausibly involves more than one of them at different points. What they share is the complete absence of any role for a distant, unrelated, and at the time nonexistent hominin lineage.

Ergot's Long Shadow

Ergot alkaloids make the same point even more sharply, because their evolutionary footprint is unusually well documented and their route into human affairs is unusually direct. Claviceps purpurea and its relatives are fungal pathogens of cereal grasses, most famously rye, that replace a developing grain with a dark, alkaloid-laden sclerotium. The alkaloids themselves are structurally diverse, built around a shared ergoline core but branching into dozens of distinct natural compounds, and that diversity is generally read as evidence of an ongoing evolutionary arms race against a broad and shifting set of consumers rather than a single fixed pest. The alkaloids affect several neurotransmitter systems at once, serotonergic, dopaminergic, and noradrenergic, and their documented ecological role is protecting the fungus, and by extension its host plant, from being eaten by both vertebrate and invertebrate herbivores. Closely related endophytic fungi in the genus Epichloë, which live inside grasses without killing them, produce an overlapping toolkit of alkaloids specialised against insects and against larger grazing mammals respectively, underlining that the target audience for this chemistry was herbivore pressure on grasses, not human physiology or human neurochemistry.

Humans nonetheless collided with ergot's chemistry early and, for centuries, involuntarily. Ergot-contaminated rye caused recurring epidemics of ergotism in medieval and early modern Europe, known as St Anthony's Fire after the monastic order that cared for sufferers; the alkaloids' vasoconstrictive action could cut off blood flow to the extremities and cause gangrene, while other alkaloids in the mixture produced convulsions and hallucination. A once-popular hypothesis connected ergotism to the 1692 Salem witch trials, but the fit is weak: contemporaneous accounts describe symptoms that varied within the same household in ways ergotism should not produce, and the specific pattern of afflictions reported does not sit comfortably with convulsive ergotism's known course, so most historians and toxicologists now treat the connection as unproven at best. What is not in dispute is that ergot alkaloids, entirely without regard for human welfare, were potent enough to reshape European folk medicine and folklore around a fungus nobody had bred, selected, or in any sense invited into that role.

The same chemistry was eventually put to deliberate use. Midwives used ergot preparations for centuries to induce uterine contractions and to control bleeding after childbirth, a use later formalised into ergometrine and related obstetric drugs, and ergotamine and its derivatives remain in use against migraine. It was in exactly this pharmaceutical context that ergot alkaloids produced their most consequential accident. In 1938, working at Sandoz on a series of ergot-derived compounds screened for circulatory and respiratory stimulant properties, the chemist Albert Hofmann synthesised lysergic acid diethylamide, compound twenty-five in that series, and set it aside as pharmacologically unremarkable. Five years later, resynthesising the compound for further testing, he absorbed a small amount through his skin and experienced its effects for the first time, an accident he followed three days later, on 19 April 1943, with an intentional self-administered dose. The ergoline scaffold that a rye pathogen had spent millions of years refining as an anti-herbivore weapon had, by a chain of events with no adaptive logic behind it at any step, become the twentieth century's most famous psychedelic.

Mapping the Receptor

Knowing that these molecules bind 5-HT2A is a pharmacological fact that predates modern structural biology by decades. Seeing exactly how they bind it is much more recent. The first high-resolution structure of 5-HT2A captured in an activated, hallucinogen-bound state, resolved by cryo-electron microscopy and reported by Kim and colleagues in Cell in 2020, showed the receptor coupled to its principal downstream partner, the Gq protein, with a synthetic hallucinogen locked into the binding pocket. That structure gave the field its first direct look at how the receptor's transmembrane helices rearrange on activation and which residues contact an agonist, rather than the pocket's shape as inferred indirectly from mutagenesis and binding assays.

Structural coverage has since broadened considerably. Cryo-EM structures of the classic tryptamine hallucinogens, serotonin itself, psilocin, and DMT, bound to 5-HT2A show these molecules forming an ionic interaction with an aspartate residue, D155, and contacting a second residue, S242, that is conserved in primates but not universally across mammals, a detail that helps explain some cross-species differences in sensitivity. A 2025 study extended the structural catalogue to seven cryo-EM structures spanning essentially every major chemotype of 5-HT2A agonist, hallucinogenic and non-hallucinogenic tryptamines, ergolines, and phenethylamines alike, plus a deliberately biased experimental compound, and found both shared and chemotype-specific binding motifs, evidence that the receptor does not have one single "on" conformation so much as a family of related ones. A separate 2025 study went further still, resolving 5-HT2A in complex with both Gq and Gi, its two principal signalling partners, and reported that Gi-mediated signalling, not Gq alone, is required for the hallucinogenic effect itself, a genuine revision of the field's earlier, simpler Gq-centred model.

A parallel line of pharmacological work has sharpened exactly what "activation" needs to mean for a compound to be hallucinogenic in practice. 5-HT2A signals through at least two distinct downstream pathways, Gq-mediated phospholipase C signalling and a separate, arrestin-mediated pathway governing receptor internalisation. Comparing a large panel of 5-HT2A agonists against a standard behavioural proxy for hallucinogenic potential in rodents, the head-twitch response, found that Gq recruitment efficacy predicts that potential closely, while arrestin recruitment does not, and that a compound needs to clear a minimum threshold of Gq efficacy before any hallucinogenic-like effect appears at all. Lisuride is the sharpest illustration of the point: it binds 5-HT2A with an affinity comparable to LSD's, occupying much the same fraction of receptors at a given concentration, and yet it is not hallucinogenic in humans, because its efficacy at driving Gq signalling once bound falls below that threshold. Binding is necessary. It is not sufficient.

Mescaline needs roughly a thousand times the concentration of LSD to reach comparable occupancy, which is exactly why a recreational or ritual mescaline dose is measured in hundreds of milligrams where an LSD dose is measured in micrograms.

The therapeutic payoff of this structural work is direct. If Gq efficacy, rather than mere occupancy, is what produces the subjective psychedelic effect, and if that effect can be dissociated from the receptor's other, potentially therapeutic downstream consequences, then a rationally designed biased agonist could in principle deliver 5-HT2A-dependent antidepressant or plasticity-promoting effects without the hallucinogenic experience at all, or with it substantially reduced. Several groups are pursuing exactly that, and lisuride's own decades-old pharmacology is now read as an existence proof that the dissociation is achievable rather than merely theoretical.

From an Accident to a Society

None of the biology above stopped this coincidence from becoming a substantial social and religious fact for the humans on the receiving end of it. Psilocybe mushrooms had been used ceremonially in Mesoamerica for centuries under the Nahuatl name teonanácatl before they became known to the West by that route: in 1955 the ethnomycologist R. Gordon Wasson participated in a nocturnal mushroom ceremony, a velada, conducted by the Mazatec curandera María Sabina in Huautla de Jiménez, Oaxaca, and his account of the experience, "Seeking the Magic Mushroom", appeared in Life magazine in 1957 to a large general readership. Samples Wasson brought back reached Albert Hofmann's laboratory at Sandoz, the same laboratory where LSD had originated, and in 1958 Hofmann isolated and determined the structure of psilocybin and psilocin, closing an unlikely loop between a Swiss ergot chemist and a Mazatec healer.

What followed in the West was rapid and, in retrospect, badly paced. Timothy Leary's Harvard Psilocybin Project and the wider counterculture of the 1960s took up both LSD and psilocybin as instruments of a self-consciously transformative social project, well ahead of any settled clinical or safety evidence, and the backlash arrived on a similar timescale: the United States' Controlled Substances Act of 1970 and the United Nations Convention on Psychotropic Substances the following year placed LSD and psilocybin in the most restrictive scheduling categories available, and clinical research on both compounds largely stopped for the following three decades. A chemical accident that had briefly reorganised religious ceremony, popular culture, and drug policy within a single generation was, by the early 1970s, treated in law as though it had no legitimate use of any kind.

That freeze has been thawing for roughly the last fifteen years, and considerably faster over the last five. The United States' Food and Drug Administration has granted Breakthrough Therapy designation to psilocybin for both treatment-resistant and major depressive disorder, a status reserved for treatments showing preliminary evidence of substantial improvement over existing options, and priority review vouchers have followed for psilocybin in depression and for methylone in PTSD. A 2025 randomised trial of single-dose psilocybin in patients with severe, previously treatment-resistant depression, published in the American Journal of Psychiatry, reported significant antidepressant effects persisting to six weeks, and separate reports have described remission sustained at six months in over half of treated patients in some psilocybin depression trials. A 2025 Phase 2 trial found a single 25 mg psilocybin dose improved PTSD symptoms for up to twelve weeks. The picture is not uniformly favourable: MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD produced positive results across two Phase 3 trials, yet the FDA declined to approve the corresponding New Drug Application in August 2024, a reminder that promising trial data and regulatory approval remain distinct hurdles. At the state level, Oregon's Measure 109 established a licensed framework for supervised psilocybin therapy, implemented from 2023, and Colorado's Natural Medicine Health Act extended a broadly similar model to a wider set of natural psychedelics. Half a century after prohibition, the same molecular coincidence that unsettled 1960s drug policy is now being investigated, cautiously and with regulatory friction, as medicine.

A Coincidence Worth Taking Seriously

Nothing in this account requires any of the organisms involved to have had humans in view. A rye pathogen's chemical defence against being eaten, refined over a period of evolutionary time with no reference to primates whatsoever, ended up as a compound a Swiss chemist would synthesise on a laboratory bench six decades before that compound reorganised a generation's culture and drug policy. A mushroom genus defending its fruiting bodies against gastropods or insects, on a timescale of tens of millions of years, ended up the object of a Mazatec religious practice and, later, a randomised controlled trial. The thread connecting all of it is not intention on the fungus's part, or design on nature's part, but the sheer antiquity and conservation of a single receptor family that was already ancient before any of these lineages existed to exploit it. Structural biology can now show, almost literally, how the key sits in that lock, down to individual contact residues and the conformational difference between a bound receptor that signals hallucination and one that does not. What it cannot do, and does not need to do, is supply a purpose behind the fit. There isn't one. The coincidence is the whole story, and it happens to be a good enough story to be building a clinical research programme on.

Further Reading

This article is a synthesis rather than a primary source. David Nichols' review article "Psychedelics" (Pharmacological Reviews) remains a widely cited survey of classic psychedelic pharmacology. Kim et al.'s 2020 Cell paper reporting the first Gq-coupled, hallucinogen-bound 5-HT2A structure is the starting point for the structural biology discussed above; the subsequent 2025 cryo-EM studies in Nature Communications and Nature extend that structural picture across chemotypes and signalling partners. On the receptor-signalling side, the Nature Communications work identifying Gq efficacy, rather than arrestin recruitment, as the correlate of psychedelic potential is the primary source for the affinity-versus-efficacy distinction the interactive figure above illustrates. On the evolutionary side, recent work on the ecology and diversification of Psilocybe, including the gastropod-defence hypothesis, and the broader literature on ergot alkaloid genetics and evolution are good starting points for the defensive-chemistry material. None of these are cited for individual sentences above: treat this as a reading list for orientation, not a citation apparatus.
