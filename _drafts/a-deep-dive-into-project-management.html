Project management is the discipline of coordinating interdependent work under real constraints: time, cost, scope, and the availability of specific people, toward an outcome that was, at the start, at least partly uncertain. Stripped of the certification-industry vocabulary that has grown up around it, most of what works reduces to a small number of ideas with real content: how to sequence dependent work, how to estimate under uncertainty, how to decide how much structure a given project needs, and how to protect a schedule from the specific, well-documented ways people misbehave around deadlines. This piece works through the major methodologies on their merits, examines what problem each one solves, and sets out how to choose between them on merit, since fashion is a poor guide.

The iron triangle, and why it undersells the problem

The classic framing is the iron triangle: scope, time, and cost, with quality as the implicit fourth corner that suffers when the other three are fixed rigidly and something has to give. This is a useful first approximation. It treats a project as a fixed bundle of work to be delivered, when what needs managing is an uncertain process. It says nothing about risk (the probability-weighted cost of things that haven't happened yet but might), or about resource contention: the same senior engineer cannot be simultaneously critical to two projects' schedules, no matter how the budget spreadsheet allocates their time on paper. A more honest model treats scope, time, cost, quality, risk, and resource availability as six variables under simultaneous constraint. Fixing more of them than the underlying uncertainty allows guarantees that reality will violate at least one of them later, usually the one nobody was watching.

Waterfall and the critical path method

Sequential-phase planning (requirements, then design, then build, then test, then deploy, each phase gated on the previous one's completion) gets treated as old-fashioned in a lot of contemporary writing, which mistakes a tool's misapplication for a flaw in the tool. Waterfall is the right choice exactly when uncertainty is low and rework is expensive: constructing a building, launching a spacecraft, anything gated by regulatory sign-off where the sequence of approvals is externally fixed and revisiting an earlier phase costs two months, not two days. Its core scheduling technique, the critical path method (CPM, Kelley and Walker, 1959), is worth knowing on its own merits regardless of methodology: model the project as a directed graph of tasks with dependencies, compute the earliest possible start and finish for every task by working forward through the graph, then the latest possible start and finish that still hits the deadline by working backward from it. The difference between those two numbers for a given task is its float (or slack): how much that task can slip without delaying the whole project. Tasks with zero float form the critical path, the longest dependency chain through the network, and the only chain whose delay is guaranteed to delay the end date. The single most common project-management error at the tactical level is spending management attention on whichever task looks most behind schedule, when the task that matters is whichever one sits on the critical path. A task with three weeks of float can slip by two weeks and the project still finishes on time; a critical-path task slipping by one day pushes the whole delivery date by one day, full stop.

PERT (Program Evaluation and Review Technique, developed alongside CPM for the U.S. Navy's Polaris programme) treats task duration as uncertain, not as a single confident number. For each task, gather three estimates: optimistic \( o \), most likely \( m \), pessimistic \( p \), and combine them into an expected duration using a Beta-distribution approximation,

\[ t_e = \frac{o + 4m + p}{6}, \qquad \sigma = \frac{p - o}{6}, \]

which weights the "most likely" estimate four times as heavily as either extreme, while still letting the spread between optimistic and pessimistic estimates feed into a genuine variance, \( \sigma^2 \), for the task. Summing variances along the critical path (assuming task durations are independent, a simplifying assumption worth stating openly) gives a variance for the whole project's completion date. That turns "the project will finish around March 14th" into the far more honest "the project has a 50% chance of finishing by March 14th, and a 90% chance of finishing by March 28th": a more useful kind of statement.

Resource levelling: the assumption CPM quietly makes

The critical path method, worked through above, computes float and the critical path on the assumption that every task can start the moment its dependencies finish. That assumption quietly requires whoever needs to do the task to be free at that moment. Real projects violate this constantly: the same specialist is the named resource on tasks B and E above, both scheduled to start in week three, and no amount of dependency logic makes that person available in two places at once. Resource levelling is the step CPM alone doesn't do: given a fixed pool of people (or equipment, or any other constrained resource) and their individual capacities, reschedule tasks that would otherwise overlap onto the same constrained resource. This frequently pushes the calculated finish date later than the pure dependency-network critical path suggested, producing a second, usually longer critical path, sometimes called the resource-critical path. It's the reason a schedule that looks achievable on a dependency diagram alone can still slip the moment it meets the reality of who is available when, especially on projects where a small number of senior specialists are load-bearing across many nominally-parallel work streams at once. A schedule that hasn't been resource-levelled hasn't been tested against the constraint most likely to break it.

Agile and Scrum: iterating against uncertainty

Where waterfall assumes requirements are knowable in advance, agile methodologies start from the opposite assumption: for a large class of work, especially software, requirements are discovered through the act of building and getting feedback. A plan that assumes otherwise only sounds more rigorous; in practice it's wrong, in a way that won't become visible until it's expensive. Scrum structures this into fixed-length iterations (sprints, typically one to four weeks), a prioritised backlog of work the team pulls from, and a small set of regular checkpoints: sprint planning, a daily stand-up, a sprint review demonstrating what was built, and a retrospective examining how the team worked as well as what it shipped. The mechanism doing the real work is the short feedback loop: a wrong assumption about what users need surfaces after one or two weeks of work, not after the six-month waterfall phase that assumed it away. That's precisely why agile approaches outperform sequential planning when requirements uncertainty is high, and offer no particular advantage, just considerable process overhead, when it isn't.

Kanban, flow, and Little's Law

Kanban takes a different cut at the same underlying problem: it visualises work as it flows through stages (a board with columns like "to do," "in progress," "review," "done") instead of batching it into fixed-length sprints, and imposes an explicit limit on how many items are allowed to sit in each in-progress column at once: a work-in-progress (WIP) limit. The reasoning has clean quantitative backing in Little's Law, a result from queueing theory (John Little, 1961) that holds for any stable system regardless of its internal details:

\[ L = \lambda W, \]

where \( L \) is the average number of items in the system (work in progress), \( \lambda \) is the average arrival (or completion) rate, and \( W \) is the average time an item spends in the system (cycle time). Rearranged, \( W = L / \lambda \): for a fixed throughput, cycle time is directly proportional to work in progress. That's the formal reason behind "start less, finish more": a team juggling twice as many concurrent tasks, at the same completion rate, takes twice as long to finish any given one of them, purely as a matter of queueing arithmetic, before context-switching cost makes the real number worse still.

Stakeholder management and the RACI matrix

Every other technique in this piece manages the work. This one manages the people who have a stake in the work without necessarily doing any of it, and neglecting it is a common, entirely separate way for a well-scheduled project to fail. A RACI matrix names, for each significant decision or deliverable, exactly who is Responsible (does the work), Accountable (owns the outcome and has final sign-off; exactly one person, never a committee, since "everyone's accountable" quietly becomes "no one is"), Consulted (input sought before a decision, two-way communication), and Informed (told after the fact, one-way communication). It sounds like bureaucratic overhead until you watch the alternative play out in practice: a decision stalls because three people each assumed someone else had authority to make it, or a stakeholder who needed to be consulted first learns about a decision only after it shipped and, entirely reasonably, escalates. The failure mode RACI prevents is unclear decision rights: the specific, expensive pattern of rework and trust damage that follows when nobody knows who owns a call. A project with an uncertain scope needs this discipline even more than a simple one, precisely because uncertain scope means more decisions have to get made on the fly.

Critical chain and the discipline of buffers

Eliyahu Goldratt's critical chain project management (1997), built on his Theory of Constraints, starts from an uncomfortable observation about how people behave around estimates, which routinely differs from how they're assumed to behave. Individual task estimates are typically padded with a personal safety margin, and that padding is routinely wasted through two well-documented effects: Parkinson's law (work expands to fill the time available, so a task that needs five days but is estimated at seven, with two days of padding built in, still takes the full seven) and student syndrome (work on a task is deferred until the deadline is close enough to create urgency, burning the safety margin before the work even starts, regardless of how much of it exists). Padding every individual task therefore doesn't protect the schedule; it just inflates it, since the padding gets consumed by behaviour, not by genuine variance. Critical chain's fix is to strip the padding out of individual task estimates (using something closer to a 50%-confidence duration) and pool it instead into shared buffers placed at strategic points: a project buffer at the end of the critical chain, and feeding buffers wherever a non-critical chain of tasks merges into the critical one, protecting it from upstream delays. The team is then managed against buffer consumption, not individual task deadlines: a task running long is only a genuine problem when it's eating meaningfully into the shared buffer. That's a more honest signal than a sea of individually red or green task-level status indicators, which can't distinguish a critical-path problem from harmless noise.

Earned value management: telling on-time-but-over-budget apart from on-budget-but-late

A project can be exactly on schedule while badly overspent, or exactly on budget while badly behind. "Percent of the budget spent so far" alone cannot distinguish the two, because it says nothing about how much work that spend bought. Earned value management (EVM) fixes this by tracking three numbers, all expressed in the same currency so they're directly comparable: Planned Value (\( PV \), the budgeted cost of the work that was scheduled to be done by now), Earned Value (\( EV \), the budgeted cost of the work actually completed by now: what it was budgeted to cost, not what it actually cost), and Actual Cost (\( AC \), what has been spent so far). Two ratios then separate the schedule question from the cost question cleanly:

\[ CPI = \frac{EV}{AC} \qquad \text{(cost performance: } >1 \text{ under budget, } <1 \text{ over)}, \qquad SPI = \frac{EV}{PV} \qquad \text{(schedule performance: } >1 \text{ ahead, } <1 \text{ behind)}. \]

A project can be simultaneously behind schedule and under budget (SPI below 1, CPI above 1: work is going slower than planned, but what has been done was done efficiently), a pattern a simple "% spent vs.\ % time elapsed" comparison would misread entirely. Projected forward, the Estimate at Completion, \( EAC = BAC / CPI \) (where \( BAC \), Budget at Completion, is the total approved budget), gives an early, honest forecast of the final cost if current cost efficiency holds. Because it's available early in the project, not only in hindsight, it's one of the few tools that can surface a budget overrun while there's still enough runway left to do something about it, instead of surfacing it only at the post-mortem.

Risk management: qualitative registers and quantitative simulation

A risk register (each identified risk scored by probability and impact, typically on a simple ordinal scale, then prioritised by the product of the two) is the standard qualitative starting point. It's useful mainly for surfacing risks early and assigning an owner to watch and mitigate each one; the number it produces shouldn't be trusted with much precision. The quantitative step up is Monte Carlo schedule simulation: assign each task a duration distribution (the PERT three-point estimate above is a natural input), then simulate the entire project thousands of times, each run sampling a random duration for every task and propagating it through the dependency network to a simulated finish date. The output is a full probability distribution over completion dates, which is what answers the question a stakeholder is asking when they say "when will this be done": a curve, not a single date treated as certain, and a defensible answer to "what date should I commit to if I want 85% confidence of hitting it."

Retrospectives and learning between projects

Scrum's retrospective is the best-known instance of a more general practice that matters regardless of methodology: a structured, blame-light review of how the work went, held close enough to the events that people still remember the specifics, and aimed at process, not individual performance: "why did our estimates for integration work run consistently short" instead of "who estimated wrong." The discipline that turns this from a one-off feel-good exercise into genuine organisational learning is a lessons-learned repository that later projects consult during their own planning phase, an active reference, not an archived document nobody reopens. An organisation that runs excellent retrospectives on every project but starts each new one from a blank page is repeating the same category of mistake in a new venue every time, just with a well-documented paper trail of having done so before. The single highest-value habit here is deceptively narrow: track the gap between estimated and actual duration by task type, pooling the data across every project instead of treating each post-mortem in isolation. That gap is usually systematic (a particular kind of integration work, a particular category of external dependency), not random, and a systematic bias is the kind of thing a future estimate can and should correct for once it's been measured even a few times.

Choosing a methodology: matching structure to uncertainty

The Cynefin framework (Dave Snowden, 1999) and the closely related Stacey matrix both make the same underlying point in slightly different vocabulary: the right amount of process structure is a function of how well understood the problem is. Organisational preference and which methodology happens to be fashionable shouldn't come into it. For simple/clear problems, where cause and effect are well understood and repeatable, heavyweight process is pure overhead: follow the known best practice. For complicated problems, where cause and effect are knowable but require real expertise to work out (most well-scoped engineering and construction projects), waterfall-style upfront planning and critical-path scheduling earn their keep, because the planning can be done accurately in advance. For complex problems, where cause and effect are only clear in retrospect and requirements can't be nailed down upfront (most novel software, most research-adjacent work), agile's short feedback loops do real work that no amount of better upfront planning could substitute for, because the information the plan would need doesn't exist yet. For chaotic problems (a genuine incident, a crisis response), the right move is to act first to stabilise the situation and only then start analysing, since there isn't time to plan and the priority is stopping things from getting worse. Applying complicated-problem tools (rigid upfront Gantt charts) to a complex problem produces a plan that is confidently, precisely wrong; applying complex-problem tools (loose, iterative sprints) to a complicated problem produces unnecessary churn on work that was, in fact, knowable in advance. Most methodology disputes are, underneath the tribal branding, disagreements about which quadrant a given project sits in.

Where PRINCE2 and PMBOK fit

It's worth placing the two dominant certification frameworks against everything above. Neither is a rival methodology to CPM, Agile, or Kanban; both function as a governance layer that can sit on top of any of them. The Project Management Institute's PMBOK (A Guide to the Project Management Body of Knowledge) is best understood as a reference taxonomy of knowledge areas and processes, scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk, procurement, stakeholder management, and more: a checklist of concerns a competent project manager should have a considered answer for, not a prescriptive sequence of steps every project must follow identically. PRINCE2 (PRojects IN Controlled Environments, originating in UK government IT projects) is more prescriptive about structure: defined stages with mandatory management review points between them, a named business case that has to remain valid throughout the project or the project is formally required to stop, and clearly defined roles (a project board, a project manager, team managers) with specific authorities attached to each. Both frameworks are compatible with waterfall or agile execution underneath them: PRINCE2 projects run agile sprints inside PRINCE2-defined stages often enough that "PRINCE2 Agile" is now an official certification variant. The actual value either provides is standardised vocabulary and a governance checklist that scales across an organisation running many projects at once, not a substitute for the scheduling, estimation, and risk techniques covered above. Both frameworks reference those techniques; neither replaces them.

What the job is

Underneath all of the specific techniques, a project manager's actual job is protecting the team's attention: surfacing the one dependency that will block the deadline before it does, distinguishing real signal (a critical-path task slipping, a buffer being consumed faster than planned) from noise (any of a hundred non-critical tasks running a day long), and making the project's actual uncertainty visible instead of papering over it with a single confident date nobody believes. The specific methodology matters far less than getting that underlying judgement right. That's why the same person who runs a rigorous, waterfall-scheduled hardware programme well can usually run a Scrum team well too, and why the reverse, someone who has only ever memorised one methodology's ceremonies, usually can't do either once the project stops looking like the textbook case.
