Smoked or injected N,N-dimethyltryptamine produces one of the most abrupt and reliably strange transitions in the pharmacology of consciousness: within a minute, the ordinary sense of a stable surrounding world can give way to what a large proportion of users describe as an arrival in an apparently autonomous realm, often populated by intelligent, communicative beings. Terence McKenna spent two decades trying to explain what he had met there; Rick Strassman spent five years giving the drug to sixty volunteers under laboratory conditions and getting strikingly similar reports back. Neither fully explained it, and the questions they raised, about what produces the experience, why its content is so consistent, and what it is reasonable to conclude from the fact that most people who have it come away convinced something real happened, remain open. This traces that territory from McKenna's hyperspace through Strassman's clinic, the contested science of endogenous DMT, and the online communities that have since supplied most of the field's data, to what the evidence actually says about the spiritual and religious interpretations so many experiencers reach for.

McKenna and the Machine Elves

Terence McKenna, an ethnobotanist, writer, and lecturer who died in 2000, gave the DMT entity phenomenon its most durable name. McKenna traced his own first encounter to smoking synthesised DMT as a student in Berkeley in 1965, though it was his 1989 book True Hallucinations that gave the experience wide circulation: he described "bursting into a space inhabited by merry elfin, self-transforming, machine creatures", small beings he later called "self-transforming machine elves" or, more casually, "tykes". In his account they greeted him, addressed him in a form of visual, non-verbal language that felt closer to song than speech, and produced a continuous stream of small manipulable objects, a display he took as evidence of some kind of autonomous, intelligent, linguistic order accessible through the drug rather than generated by it. He was characteristically candid about not knowing what to make of this: "I don't know why there should be an invisible syntactical intelligence giving language lessons in hyperspace."

McKenna's standing as a source is complicated by the company his DMT reports kept in his wider body of work. Food of the Gods (1992) advanced the "stoned ape" hypothesis, that dietary psilocybin drove a cognitive leap in early hominins, an argument that has found little support and is generally considered to rest on a misreading of the primate behavioural literature it cites. Timewave Zero, a numerological system McKenna developed with his brother Dennis from patterns in the I Ching, purported to chart the ebb and flow of "novelty" through history and predicted a singularity on 21 December 2012; the date passed without incident and the theory is now treated as a curiosity rather than a serious model. None of this invalidates McKenna's DMT reports, which are broadly consistent with what later, methodologically controlled research would find, but it is a reason to treat him as a vivid primary witness and populariser rather than as an authority on what the experience means, a distinction worth keeping in mind given how much of the field's own history, covered in a companion piece on this site, turns on that kind of conflation.

Strassman's Volunteers

Rick Strassman ran the clinical research that gave McKenna's anecdotal reports something closer to a data set. Between 1990 and 1995, in the FDA-approved University of New Mexico study that reopened human hallucinogen research in the United States after two decades, discussed in more detail in a companion piece on this site, Strassman administered roughly four hundred doses of intravenous DMT to sixty volunteers. A substantial share of them reported convincing encounters with autonomous, apparently intelligent presences, variously described as insectoid, reptilian, robotic, clown-like, or elf-like, operating in a space that felt, to the volunteers, at least as real as ordinary waking life rather than like a dream or a hallucination they could easily dismiss. Strassman published the results as the clinical monograph they were, but his 2001 popular book, DMT: The Spirit Molecule, went further, proposing that the pineal gland might secrete endogenous DMT at pharmacologically significant moments, birth, dreaming, and death among them, and that this could underlie naturally occurring near-death and mystical experiences as well as his volunteers' reports.

Is There Really a Pineal "Spirit Molecule"?

The endogenous-DMT hypothesis has not held up well in the specific form Strassman proposed it, though the underlying chemistry turns out to be more interesting than a simple debunking would suggest. David Nichols' 2018 review, "N,N-dimethyltryptamine and the pineal gland: separating fact from myth", is the clearest statement of the case against a pineal "spirit molecule": the adult human pineal gland weighs under 0.2 grams and produces around 30 micrograms of melatonin a day, and Nichols argues it is not plausible for an organ of that size to synthesise and release anything close to the roughly 25 milligrams of DMT that would be needed to provoke a psychedelic experience, however briefly. That is a substantial mismatch in scale, and it is the strongest reason for scepticism about Strassman's original framing.

It is not, however, evidence that DMT is absent from mammalian brain tissue. Jimo Borjigin and colleagues detected DMT directly in rat pineal microdialysate in 2013, and a 2019 follow-up found the enzymes needed to synthesise it, and DMT itself, distributed well beyond the pineal gland, including in the neocortex and hippocampus, at extracellular concentrations comparable to established monoamine neurotransmitters such as serotonin, and persisting even after the pineal gland was surgically removed. The same study found DMT levels rising in the visual cortex of rats undergoing experimentally induced cardiac arrest. So the underlying claim, that mammalian brains synthesise DMT, is now reasonably well supported. What remains unestablished is the much stronger claim Strassman and McKenna's popular reception both leaned on, that this endogenous DMT reaches concentrations sufficient to produce or explain psychedelic, near-death, or mystical states under any normal or even extreme physiological circumstance. That connection is still open, and treating it as settled would repeat a pattern this field has been burned by before.

Modelling the Near-Death Experience

Rather than resolve the endogenous question, more recent research has tested Strassman's near-death parallel from the other direction, by asking how closely an exogenous DMT experience resembles a reported near-death experience. Christopher Timmermann and colleagues at Imperial College London ran a placebo-controlled, within-subjects study, published in 2018 as "DMT Models the Near-Death Experience", giving thirteen healthy volunteers DMT and inert placebo and comparing their reports against standard near-death experience phenomenology; the DMT sessions produced significantly higher scores on near-death-experience measures than placebo, with substantial overlap across nearly all of the individual phenomenological features surveyors use to characterise reported near-death experiences.

A practical limitation of this work is that a smoked or injected DMT experience is extremely brief, typically resolving within about fifteen minutes, which leaves little time to study its content in detail. Timmermann's group addressed this directly with an extended-state DMT protocol, informally called DMT-X, reported in 2023: eleven volunteers received a bolus dose followed by a continuous low-rate intravenous infusion over thirty minutes, sustaining a stable psychedelic state well beyond the normal window while remaining safe and well tolerated. Subjective effects began roughly a minute after the initial dose, peaked within two minutes, and resolved within about twenty minutes of the infusion ending, giving researchers, for the first time, an experimentally extended and controllable version of the state McKenna and Strassman's volunteers had only been able to describe in retrospect.

The Psychonauts and Their Archive

Alongside the clinical literature, a parallel and considerably larger body of DMT reports has accumulated outside any laboratory, generated by self-experimenters usually described, using a term coined by the German writer Ernst Jünger in 1970 to describe the pharmacologist Arthur Heffter's self-administered mescaline research, as psychonauts: literally, sailors of the mind. Erowid, founded in 1995 by Earth and Fire Erowid as a non-profit archive of drug information and first-person experience, has published more than thirty thousand reviewed trip reports covering DMT and every other substance discussed on this site, built specifically to preserve exactly the kind of naturalistic, unsupervised account that a clinical trial's inclusion criteria and dosing protocol necessarily exclude.

That self-reported material has begun feeding back into the peer-reviewed literature directly rather than remaining a separate, informal tradition. David Wyndham Lawrence, Robin Carhart-Harris, Roland Griffiths, and Christopher Timmermann's 2022 study in Scientific Reports analysed 3,778 DMT experiences posted to the r/DMT subreddit between 2009 and 2018, treating the community's own trip reports as a large-scale, naturalistic dataset. They found entities described in 45.5 per cent of reports, characterised in recurring types, feminine-presenting figures, deities, alien-like beings, creature-based entities, mythological figures, and jester or clown-like figures, alongside somatic effects and visual content broadly consistent with the smaller clinical samples. This is a useful source of scale and ecological validity that no funded trial could match, but it carries the obvious limitations of self-selected, unblinded, uncontrolled-dose reporting, and it is worth reading the psychonaut archive and the clinical literature as complementary rather than interchangeable: one supplies breadth and authenticity of context, the other supplies the dosing control and comparison conditions needed to draw causal conclusions.

Spiritual and Religious Interpretation

However the experience is generated, a large fraction of people who have it interpret it in explicitly spiritual or religious terms, and that interpretation has itself become a subject of survey research. Roland Griffiths and colleagues' 2019 study in PLOS ONE surveyed 4,285 people about a memorable "God encounter experience", split across those with no drug involved and those occasioned by psilocybin, LSD, ayahuasca, or DMT. Among respondents who identified as atheist before the experience, a clear majority no longer did afterward, 74 per cent in the non-drug group and 67 per cent in the psychedelic-occasioned group, a shift the authors found statistically significant across every substance studied. The psychedelic-occasioned group leaned toward more abstract, less anthropomorphic language than the non-drug group: 55 per cent described the encountered presence as "Ultimate Reality" against 26 per cent of the non-drug group, while the non-drug group more often reached for "God" outright, 41 per cent against 18 per cent. Roughly nine in ten respondents in both groups reported a strong emotional response, and about two-thirds reported some form of communicative exchange with the entity or presence. DMT and ayahuasca produced a distinctly different profile from psilocybin and LSD, which were themselves nearly indistinguishable from one another: DMT and ayahuasca sessions scored higher on standard mystical-experience measures, involved more explicit entity contact with a stronger sense of the entity's own intentionality, and ayahuasca respondents in particular were the most likely to describe the presence as benevolent, conscious, and intelligent, and reported the strongest lasting improvements in life satisfaction, relationships, and sense of spiritual meaning.

The instrument behind much of this comparative work, the Mystical Experience Questionnaire, descends from Walter Pahnke's 1962 Good Friday Experiment, discussed at length in a companion piece on this site, which built its eight scoring categories directly from the philosopher W.T. Stace's typology of mystical states: unity, transcendence of time and space, sacredness, a sense of objective reality, deeply felt positive mood, ineffability, paradoxicality, and transiency. DMT sessions reliably score at or above psilocybin and LSD sessions on this instrument, with a higher proportion of participants meeting the threshold for a "complete" mystical experience, even though the entity-contact character of a typical DMT session sits somewhat awkwardly against Stace's original, more classically unitive conception of mysticism. Whether an encounter with a communicative, apparently autonomous being is best classified alongside undifferentiated unitive experience, or treated as a related but phenomenologically distinct category, is an open question in the literature rather than a settled one. David Luke's 2011 review, "Discarnate entities and dimethyltryptamine (DMT): psychopharmacology, phenomenology and ontology", is the clearest attempt to hold that question open deliberately, surveying the parapsychological, ethnobotanical, and pharmacological literature side by side without either endorsing the entities' independent existence or dismissing the question as obviously closed. That restraint is the right academic posture here: the consistency and intensity of the reports are well documented, the neuropharmacological trigger is increasingly well characterised, and the further step, from a reliably produced experience of contact to a claim about what, if anything, is actually being contacted, is not one the current evidence settles either way.

A Consistent Experience, an Open Question

Three strands of evidence now point in the same direction without quite meeting. McKenna's descriptions from the 1960s onward, Strassman's clinical sample from the 1990s, and the tens of thousands of self-reports since collected by Erowid and analysed from platforms such as Reddit converge on a strikingly consistent phenomenology: a fast onset, a sense of arrival somewhere else, and frequent, often communicative contact with beings the majority of experiencers come away believing were, in some sense, real. The pharmacology behind that convergence is real and increasingly well mapped, and the endogenous-DMT question Strassman raised has turned out to be more interesting, and less settled, than either his original hypothesis or Nichols' rebuttal alone would suggest. What has not converged, and probably should not be expected to, is agreement on what the experience means. That is a question this piece has deliberately left open rather than resolved, in keeping with a field that, as the companion pieces on this site describe, has already paid once for letting a compelling story get ahead of what the evidence could support.

Lawrence, Carhart-Harris, Griffiths, and Timmermann's 2022 Scientific Reports paper is the primary source for the r/DMT analysis, and Erowid's own experience archive is the primary source for the wider psychonaut trip-report tradition it draws on.
