This is the systems-level companion to the maths write-up on the same project: twelve fixed, unsynchronised cameras covering a factory floor (the Warehouse_014 dataset), turned into one browser-based viewer where a person keeps one identity as they walk between camera views, with an optional 3D pose reconstruction on top. The maths post covers the algorithms in full derivation; this one covers what the actual tool looks like and how the pieces fit together.

What it does

factory_explorer_temp is an interactive, no-build-step web viewer over a whole recorded dataset: browse detected people frame by frame, per camera, on a top-down factory map, with identities merged across cameras (the same physical person seen by two cameras at once gets one ID) and tracked over time (that ID follows them between frames rather than being reassigned every frame). It's a genuinely small stack — static HTML/CSS/vanilla JS talking to a small Python http.server-based backend, no framework either side — built to sit on top of an offline detection-and-tracking pipeline rather than duplicate any of its logic client-side.

The main viewer has three panels: a clickable list of numbered camera markers on the left, the selected camera's current frame in the middle (people boxed, labelled with their cross-camera global ID, and — where available — their 17 COCO body keypoints drawn directly on the footage), and a top-down "averaged positions" map on the right, one dot per identity, at the average of its position across every camera that saw them that frame. A shared frame slider and Play button scrub or auto-advance through the recording.

Identity tracking, in one paragraph

Two different problems get solved here, both in scripts/tracking/reid.py: deciding whether two cameras are looking at the same person right now (gated on both real-world proximity and appearance similarity holding at once), and deciding whether the person in front of a camera now is the same one seen a moment ago (a per-identity Kalman filter predicts where they should be, Mahalanobis-gated; an unambiguous, uncontested match continues without needing appearance evidence to confirm it; anything genuinely ambiguous falls back to Hungarian assignment over motion, appearance, and posture cost together). The full derivation — why a naive fixed-radius gate or an always-require-appearance-agreement rule both fail in different, real, observed ways on this dataset — is in the maths post; this project is the reason that derivation exists in the first place.

The other three pages

Three more pages live alongside the main viewer, each talking to its own slice of the backend rather than re-implementing anything client-side:

  • Homography calibration — pick a camera, click matching points on its frame and on the top-down map (SIFT keypoints offered as candidates, clicks snapping to the nearest one), then compute and save the camera's ground-plane homography via OpenCV's findHomography. This is what turns "a person is at this pixel in this camera" into "a person is at this real-world floor position."
  • ReID diagnostics — the page that generated the two figures above: set sampling and per-technique hyperparameters (t-SNE perplexity; UMAP neighbours/min-distance), and the server renders the plot on demand rather than the browser doing any of the actual dimensionality reduction.
  • 3D skeleton viewer — loads every identity that's already had its skeleton reconstructed offline (multi-view DLT triangulation plus Levenberg–Marquardt refinement, per the maths post) and plays them back: a big view of whichever camera's footage is selected with every visible identity's skeleton overlaid, plus one small box per identity reprojected through a fixed synthetic viewpoint so they all stay in frame regardless of where on the floor they actually are.

Why the heavy reconstruction stages are precomputed, not live

Skeleton reconstruction and the optional pixel-filled person models (real footage pixels sampled onto a point cloud around each skeleton) are both deliberately offline, precomputed-once steps, not something the server does on request. Reconstructing a whole video's worth of one identity's skeleton is thousands of nonlinear least-squares calls; running several of those inside the HTTP server's own request threads was heavy enough to crash it outright during development. Precomputing to disk and having the server just read the result back sidesteps that entirely — the live viewer's job is reprojection through a camera matrix, which is cheap, not reconstruction, which isn't.

Where identity actually gets decided

There is exactly one tracking policy in this codebase, not two: the offline batch pass (scripts/detection/person_detection.py) runs the full ReIDTracker once, in order, over the entire recorded dataset, and bakes the resulting global_id permanently into each camera's detections.json — that baked value is authoritative. The live viewer's backend reads that straight off disk for anything already tagged, and only falls back to running its own (identically constructed) tracker instance for a detection the batch pass hasn't reached yet, e.g. while it's still mid-run. The browser itself never computes or merges an identity; it only ever displays whatever global_id the backend already returned.

Stack

YOLO (Ultralytics) for person detection and pose keypoints, OpenCV for homography/calibration and image handling, SciPy for the skeleton triangulation and refinement, scikit-learn and UMAP-learn for the ReID diagnostics, a small Python http.server-based backend with no web framework, and a static HTML/CSS/vanilla-JS frontend with no build step.
